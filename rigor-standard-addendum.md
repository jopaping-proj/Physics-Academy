## Target Student Performance

Design all instructional materials for students aiming for:

- AP Physics examination score: 5
- IB Physics SL/HL subject grade: 7
- a broader academic profile consistent with students capable of achieving 42+ points in the IB Diploma Programme

Do not design lessons merely to help students pass the course or reproduce standard textbook exercises. The repository should progressively develop the level of conceptual understanding, mathematical fluency, experimental reasoning, data interpretation, representation fluency, argumentation, and transfer required of top-performing AP and IB students. The target learner should eventually be able to solve unfamiliar problems without relying on memorized templates.

## 1. Definition of Rigor

Do not equate rigor with: excessive calculation, unnecessarily difficult algebra, unusually large numbers, long problem sets, obscure tricks, or memorizing more formulas.

Define rigor primarily through the quality of thinking required. Students should progressively be required to:

1.1. identify the relevant physical principle without being told which equation to use
1.2. construct mathematical models from physical situations
1.3. translate among verbal, graphical, diagrammatic, numerical, and algebraic representations
1.4. justify claims using physical principles
1.5. distinguish observations from interpretations
1.6. evaluate assumptions
1.7. analyze limiting cases
1.8. determine whether numerical answers are physically reasonable
1.9. reason proportionally without always performing full calculations
1.10. solve unfamiliar and non-routine problems
1.11. analyze experimental data
1.12. interpret uncertainty
1.13. evaluate competing models
1.14. identify misconceptions and flawed reasoning
1.15. synthesize concepts from multiple units

## 2. Cognitive Demand Progression

Every major topic should progress through increasingly sophisticated cognitive demand, roughly:

**Level 1 — Recognition and Recall.** Students identify definitions, quantities, units, fundamental relationships, representations. Necessary but insufficient for high examination performance.

**Level 2 — Direct Application.** Students apply a familiar principle to a familiar situation. *Example: calculate acceleration when net force and mass are known.*

**Level 3 — Conceptual Reasoning.** Students explain relationships without depending exclusively on calculations. *Example: two objects experience equal net forces; one has twice the mass of the other — compare their accelerations and justify.*

**Level 4 — Multi-Representation Reasoning.** Students translate among equation, graph, vector diagram, verbal description, physical model. *Example: given a velocity-time graph, construct the acceleration-time graph and describe the motion verbally.*

**Level 5 — Multi-Step Modeling.** Students determine independently the system, relevant principles, assumptions, required intermediate quantities, and mathematical pathway. Do not tell students which equation to use.

**Level 6 — Transfer.** Students apply known principles to an unfamiliar context; surface features should differ from previously demonstrated examples.

**Level 7 — Synthesis.** Students combine concepts from multiple topics, e.g. energy + circular motion, momentum + energy, rotation + energy, electric fields + energy, SHM + energy, waves + geometry, thermal physics + mechanics.

**Level 8 — Evaluation and Argumentation.** Students critique a proposed solution, evaluate experimental evidence, compare competing claims, determine whether conclusions follow from data, and defend an answer using physics.

Lessons intended for AP 5 / IB 7 performance must regularly reach Levels 5–8.

## 3. Three-Tier Lesson Difficulty

Within each lesson, organize practice progressively:

**Foundation** — establishes essential conceptual and mathematical understanding; ensures prerequisite mastery.

**Examination Readiness** — reasoning comparable to standard AP or IB examination demand: selecting principles, multiple representations, unfamiliar contexts, justification, multi-step reasoning.

**Mastery / Distinction** — sophistication expected from students targeting the highest examination bands: non-routine applications, synthesis across concepts, experimental interpretation, error analysis, derivations, proportional reasoning, limiting-case reasoning, model evaluation.

Do not make every question maximally difficult. The progression should deliberately scaffold students toward expert-level performance.

> **Mapping to the canonical difficulty scale (§21):** these three tiers are a page-layout grouping, not a separate tagging vocabulary. Foundation groups questions tagged Foundation/Developing; Examination Readiness groups AP-IB Standard/AP5-IB7 Target; Mastery/Distinction groups Distinction-Stretch. Every individual question still gets exactly one of the five §21 labels in its data; these three names only control which visual section of the lesson it renders in.

## 4. AP Physics 5 Standard

For AP Physics materials, emphasize the scientific practices and reasoning patterns required for top AP performance.

**Representation.** Construct and interpret free-body diagrams, motion diagrams, energy bar charts, momentum representations, vector diagrams, graphs, mathematical models.

**Mathematical Reasoning.** Derive relationships; manipulate equations symbolically; use proportional reasoning; analyze functional dependence; determine graphical relationships; linearize relationships when appropriate; reason without numerical substitution.

**Experimental Design.** Identify independent and dependent variables; identify controls; propose measurements; design procedures; describe methods for reducing uncertainty; determine appropriate graphs; extract physical quantities from slopes and intercepts.

**Data Analysis.** Interpret tables and graphs; identify trends; determine relationships; estimate uncertainty; evaluate whether evidence supports a model.

**Argumentation.** Construct responses using: Claim → Relevant Physics Principle → Evidence / Mathematical Relationship → Reasoning → Conclusion. Avoid answers that merely state a result.

## 5. AP Free-Response Preparation

Throughout AP lessons, progressively train students to respond to free-response questions: derive an expression; justify a prediction; sketch and justify a graph; explain differences between two situations; design an experiment; analyze experimental results; evaluate another student's reasoning; make and defend a claim.

Whenever possible, require students to answer before revealing solutions. Solutions should model the minimum complete reasoning needed for full credit, rather than excessively long explanations.

## 6. IB Physics Grade 7 Standard

For IB Physics, design tasks consistent with the reasoning, mathematical sophistication, and communication expected of a grade-7 candidate: precise use of physics terminology, confident mathematical manipulation, dimensional awareness, graphical interpretation, experimental reasoning, quantitative modeling, awareness of assumptions, coherent extended reasoning, synthesis across topics.

For HL material, increase conceptual depth rather than merely adding more calculations.

## 7. IB Quantitative Reasoning

Students should routinely solve problems where the relevant formula is not identified for them. Require students to determine: physical principle → model → assumptions → relevant variables → symbolic relationship → calculation → interpretation.

Where appropriate, encourage symbolic solutions before numerical substitution. Include problems requiring algebraic derivation, proportional reasoning, logarithmic reasoning where relevant, vector reasoning, graphical differentiation, graphical integration, uncertainty propagation, dimensional analysis.

## 8. IB Data-Based Reasoning

Include frequent data-analysis tasks. Students should practice: determining gradients; interpreting intercepts; constructing best-fit relationships; evaluating scatter; estimating uncertainty; recognizing systematic and random error; comparing theoretical and experimental results; identifying anomalous data; evaluating the validity of conclusions.

Whenever possible, connect graphical quantities directly to physical meaning, e.g.:

- Slope of x-t → velocity
- Slope of v-t → acceleration
- Area under v-t → displacement
- Area under F-t → impulse
- Area under F-x → work

Students should understand why these relationships work mathematically and physically.

## 9. Formula Mastery Beyond Substitution

Every important formula should be taught at four levels. Example — F = ma:

**Level 1 — Meaning.** Explain what each quantity represents.

**Level 2 — Relationship.** Explore F ∝ a when mass is constant, and F ∝ m when acceleration is constant.

**Level 3 — Graphical Representation.** Investigate F vs a, F vs m, a vs F, a vs m. Students should predict graph shapes before viewing them.

**Level 4 — Transfer.** E.g. "If mass triples while net force doubles, by what factor does acceleration change?" Prefer reasoning before calculator use.

Interactive sliders should support this progression rather than function as visual decoration.

## 10. Prediction Before Simulation

For interactive simulations, require a prediction whenever pedagogically appropriate: Predict → Commit → Manipulate → Observe → Explain → Generalize.

*Example: a projectile is launched at the same speed at 30°, 45°, and 60°. Rank the horizontal ranges before running the simulation. After interacting, ask students to explain the result using horizontal velocity and time of flight.*

The simulation should test reasoning rather than replace reasoning.

## 11. Graph Mastery

Students targeting AP 5 and IB 7 should be fluent with graphs. Do not limit graph questions to reading coordinates. Require students to: determine slope; interpret physical meaning of slope; determine area; interpret physical meaning of area; identify extrema; identify turning points; compare instantaneous and average quantities; predict one graph from another; reconstruct physical motion from a graph; infer mathematical relationships; linearize nonlinear relationships.

For kinematics, regularly require transitions among x(t) ↕ v(t) ↕ a(t), understood both graphically and mathematically.

## 12. Misconception-Based Assessment

Distractors should represent authentic misconceptions whenever possible.

*Example: "A ball reaches the highest point of its trajectory. What is its acceleration?" Distractors should diagnose misconceptions such as: acceleration becomes zero; acceleration changes direction; velocity and acceleration must point in the same direction.*

Feedback should explicitly address the reasoning behind each misconception. Use diagnostic assessment to inform subsequent instruction.

## 13. Error Analysis

Frequently present incorrect student solutions.

*Example: "Student A: v = 0 at the top of the trajectory. Therefore a = 0." Ask students to: (1) identify the error, (2) explain why the reasoning is incorrect, (3) provide the correct reasoning, (4) state the correct result.*

This task type should become increasingly common in advanced lessons.

## 14. Interleaving

High-performing students must recognize principles without relying on topic labels. Regularly mix questions involving kinematics, dynamics, energy, momentum, rotation, waves, electricity, fields — do not always tell students which topic a problem belongs to.

For later units, include cumulative problems requiring students to decide which framework is most efficient (e.g. "Could this be solved using forces, energy, or both? Which approach is most efficient and why?").

## 15. Expert Strategy Development

Explicitly teach students to ask: What is the system? What interactions matter? What is conserved? What changes? What remains constant? What representation would help? What principle governs the situation? What assumptions am I making? What should the answer approximately look like? Does my final answer make physical sense?

These questions should gradually become internal habits rather than permanent scaffolds.

## 16. Fade Scaffolding

Early lessons may provide substantial guidance. Over time, deliberately remove scaffolding, progressing: fully worked example → partially worked example → hinted problem → independent problem → unfamiliar transfer problem → cross-topic synthesis problem.

Students targeting the highest examination bands must eventually operate without procedural cues.

## 17. Retrieval Standards

Retrieval questions should not only test definitions:

- **Recent Retrieval** — concepts taught within the current lesson
- **Spaced Retrieval** — concepts from earlier lessons
- **Interleaved Retrieval** — questions combining previous and current concepts
- **Generative Retrieval** — ask students to reconstruct equations, graphs, derivations, explanations before revealing them

## 18. Formative Assessment Quality Standard

A formative quiz should diagnose what the student understands, not merely produce a score. Map each question explicitly to a sub-objective. Recommended internal metadata per question: Objective, Skill, Representation, Difficulty, Misconception tested, AP/IB practice connection.

At the end of a quiz, the system should eventually be capable of reporting Mastered / Developing / Needs Review by learning objective rather than only giving a percentage score.

## 19. Lesson Exit Standard

A student should not be considered to have mastered a lesson merely because they can reproduce the examples. A lesson-level assessment should contain at least one near-transfer and one far-transfer question whenever appropriate.

*Example progression — taught: block sliding down a ramp. Near transfer: block pulled upward along a ramp. Far transfer: vehicle traveling on a banked road. The underlying physics should remain recognizable only through conceptual understanding.*

## 20. Unit-Level Mastery

At the end of each unit, provide a cumulative assessment containing conceptual reasoning, quantitative modeling, graphs, representations, experimental reasoning, error analysis, unfamiliar applications, and synthesis questions.

Do not organize the assessment into obvious subtopic blocks — students should determine which physics applies.

## 21. Examination Calibration — Canonical Difficulty Scale

**This is the single canonical difficulty vocabulary for the entire repository.** Every `difficulty` field in the question bank (`master-project-prompt.md` §27), every practice-problem tag (`master-project-prompt.md` §21), and every reference to difficulty anywhere else in either document uses exactly one of these five values — no other difficulty label should be introduced elsewhere.

When generating examination-level questions, classify them internally as: Foundation / Developing / AP-IB Standard / AP5-IB7 Target / Distinction-Stretch.

The majority of final unit-review problems for advanced students should fall into AP/IB Standard and AP 5 / IB 7 Target. Use Distinction/Stretch problems selectively — do not let olympiad-style problems distort preparation for AP or IB examinations.

## 22. Quality-Control Question

Before approving any lesson, ask:

- If a student mastered everything in this lesson, would that mastery materially improve their ability to earn an AP 5 or IB Physics 7? If the answer is no, revise the lesson.
- Does this lesson teach the student to think independently, or merely imitate worked examples? Prefer the former.

## Final Pedagogical Standard

The repository should progressively transform students from "What formula do I use?" into "What physical principle governs this situation?" and eventually into "What model best represents this system, what assumptions does that model make, what evidence supports it, and how can I determine whether my conclusion is physically reasonable?"

That level of reasoning is the target standard for the entire repository. Use verified sources only.
